Executive Summary 
Context: In the manufacturing industry, particularly within Top-Level Assembly (TLA), 
ensuring consistent quality remains a challenge. TLA involves a single operator 
assembling complex, multi-layer products ranging from mechanical to electronic 
components, where human error caused by fatigue or cognitive overload can result in 
costly rework, material wastage, and reduced operational efficiency. Traditional 
inspection techniques often fail to detect defects early, while existing AI-based CV 
systems lack the flexibility needed to handle the complexity and variability of TLA. 
 
Concept: To address this, we developed VOTÉ (Vision on the Edge), a cost-effective, 
AI-powered edge computer vision (CV) inspection system that enables real-time, 
step-by-step assembly guidance. The solution empowers quality control (QC) engineers 
to train and deploy custom CV models through a no-code desktop application, while 
operators receive guided inspection feedback via an intuitive edge-based interface. The 
system integrates Poka-Yoke principles to catch and prevent defects. 
 
Solution: We designed and iterated on a complete end-to-end solution comprising a 
compact mechanical design for a stable, non-intrusive, and customizable setup, 
alongside user-friendly interfaces for both model training and live inference. Through 
extensive user testing with Venture Corporation and the Advanced Manufacturing 
Technology Centre (AMTC), VOTÉ evolved into a robust and responsive system that 
helps improve quality control measures in the TLA industry. 
 
Outcome: Key insights included the importance of model interpretability, balanced 
datasets, and a no-code UI. Ultimately, VOTÉ demonstrated its ability to reduce 
misassemblies and false positives, while providing actionable feedback to both 
engineers and operators. By bridging the gap between advanced CV technologies and 
non-technical users, VOTÉ paves the way for scalable, accessible AI-based CV 
inspection across small and large-scale manufacturing environments.

Chapter 1 | Introduction & Problem Statement 
1.1 Problem Introduction 
Cost of quality (CoQ) [Appendix A] and defect problems in complex assemblies have 
long posed significant challenges for manufacturing companies. Not only can the 
manufacturing sites suffer from poor yield and damaged reputation due to rejected 
assemblies, high rework costs can severely impact operational profitability [1]. 
 
Figure 1: The Factor of 10 rule [2] 
 
Figure 2: 1-10-100 rule [3] 
According to the traditional Rule of Ten [Figure 1, Figure 2], CoQ due to defects 
increases exponentially at each stage of the manufacturing process. This suggests that 
defects have to be detected in early stages by implementing preventative measures, to 
reduce the compounded CoQ [4]. 
 
17

PAGE 18
============================================================
​
​
​
​
​
​
​
​
​ 
This is particularly important for Top-Level Assembly (TLA) [Appendix B], the stage 
where the final product is fully assembled before dispatch. Given its position at the end 
of the production chain, defects discovered after this stage are significantly more costly. 
This is evident in the PFMEA (Process Failure Modes and Effects Analysis) Severity 
Scale in Figure 3 which is a framework used to assess the impact of failure modes 
[Appendix C]. 
 
Figure 3: PFMEA Severity Scale in Manufacturing [5] 
On this scale, TLA processes are subjected to a severity ranking of 2 to 3, which 
indicates a low-to-moderate impact. Tackling issues at this stage is essential to prevent 
escalation into higher-severity failures and financial losses.  
 
Thus, this project aims to implement preventative measures to reduce defects in TLA 
processes to reduce the compounded CoQ.

1.2 Current Solutions, Limitations, and Root Cause Analysis 
Existing solutions can be categorized into traditional and modern approaches, with the 
former focusing on corrective measures and the latter shifting toward preventive 
strategies. 
 
Figure 4: ISO 2859-1 standard inspection protocol illustration [6] 
Traditional solutions often adhere to the ISO 2859-1 standard inspection protocol 
[Appendix D], which involves random sampling of product batches to assess their 
overall quality [6]. However, this method is limited in its effectiveness for catching all 
assembly errors, as it does not guarantee the detection of defects within every unit.  
 
Figure 5: Quality Alert Distribution in TLA [AMTC]  
 
19 

============================================================
PAGE 20
============================================================
​
​
​
​
​

Figure 5 presents the quality alert distribution observed by the Advanced Manufacturing 
Technology Centre (AMTC) at Venture when using traditional inspection methods. This 
is supported by our interview with their engineer, who noted that such methods often 
miss defects, with at least one TLA reject occurring every three days [Appendix E]. 
These findings prompted us to investigate the root causes of traditional inspection 
limitations. 
 
Figure 6: Root Cause Analysis - Undetected defects in traditional solution 
The root cause analysis diagram above illustrates why the standard inspection is not 
suitable for TLA processes inspection. The complex and multi-layer TLA product nature 
demands a method that can monitor every assembly step to detect defects, and raise 
errors whenever defects are detected to inform operators before proceeding into the 
next steps [Appendix F - Figure F9, F10]. 
 
Figure 7: Visualisation of the Poka-Yoke Technique 
To be able to detect defects at every step, the modern inspection process integrates 
Computer Vision (CV) technology with a methodology of Quality Control (QC), known as 
Poka-Yoke [7-9], to incorporate fail-safes directly into the TLA process [Appendix G]. CV 
solutions are preferred due to their superior consistency, accuracy, and efficiency in 
inspection tasks compared to traditional quality control methods [Appendix H].

Chapter 7 | Reflection and Lessons Learned 
 
During the second semester, our team encountered several key technical and 
process-related challenges that led to valuable lessons. Initially, we struggled with 
optimizing the ViT model for real-time inference due to RAM limitations on the i.MX 
platform. This forced us to explore model compression, TensorRT acceleration, and 
ultimately transition to the Jetson Orin Nano, which taught us the importance of 
hardware-software compatibility early in the design phase. We also learned that system 
performance isn’t just about accuracy, visual feedback latency and user clarity are just 
as critical.  
 
Implementing targeted overlay feedback based on classification results greatly 
improved user experience and became a turning point in our design. From a team 
perspective, we initially faced difficulty coordinating tasks, especially when hardware 
testing created bottlenecks for software development. We resolved this by breaking 
down responsibilities more granularly and aligning progress through weekly sync-ups 
and task boards.  
 
Process-wise, our early planning lacked sufficient buffer time for integration and testing. 
In hindsight, we would have reserved more time for debugging across components, as 
last-minute issues such as camera vibration and USB instability affected system 
reliability. We also learned the value of iterative testing, by constantly verifying our 
pipeline at each step, we avoided major integration failures later on.  
 
Overall, the experience sharpened our ability to balance technical ambition with 
practical constraints and reinforced the importance of adaptability, clear communication, 
and user-centered design.

Appendix A: Understanding the Cost of Quality (CoQ) [5] 
The Cost of Quality (CoQ) refers to the total cost involved in ensuring and maintaining 
product quality, including the cost of both preventing and correcting defects. Table A1 
shows the four key categories of CoQ that are related to the manufacturing industry. 
 
Table A1: Four Key Categories of CoQ 
Prevention Costs 
Investments in systems (like VOTÉ) to prevent defects during TLA 
Appraisal Costs 
Resources used to inspect or monitor product quality during assembly 
Internal Failure 
Cost of rework or scrap when defects are found before leaving the 
workstation 
External Failure 
Cost of defects found after delivery — including warranty claims and 
returns 
 
Generally, efficient CoQ management involves investing in prevention and appraisal 
efforts while minimizing the costs associated with failures.

Appendix C: Integrated FMEA ‘Severity’ scale with traditional Rule of Ten [5]

The above modified FMEA ‘Severity’ Scale is used to reflect the traditional ‘rule of ten’

increases tenfold for each stage of the process it has gone through. If a customer 
returns a product immediately, the cost to replace and process it is about equal to the

Appendix G: Poka-Yoke Explained [7-9] 
Poka-yoke is a Japanese quality control technique developed by former Toyota 
engineer Shigeo Shingo. Translated as “mistake-proofing”, poka-yoke aims to prevent 
defects in the manufacturing process that are the result of human error. Poka-yoke is a 
lean manufacturing technique that ensures that the right conditions exist before a step 
in the process is executed. This makes it a preventative form of quality control since 
errors are detected and then rectified before they occur. Figure G1 illustrates the six 
core principles of Poka-Yoke, arranged in order of decreasing effectiveness. 
 
 
Figure G1: The six principles of Poka-Yoke 
 
Poka-Yoke is especially useful in managing the Cost of Quality, which emphasizes that 
the longer a defect goes undetected, the more expensive it becomes to fix. While 
human error is unavoidable regardless of how simple or complex a task may be, Poka 
Yoke reduces the opportunities for those errors to happen. This not only improves 
product quality but also creates a less stressful working environment.

Appendix J: Calculating the Minimal Camera Pixels Requirements 
Using the thin lens equation: 
FoV = 2 * D * tan(θ/2​), where θ = 2 * arctan(S/2f​) 
Key Parameters: 
S = Sensor dimension (horizontal or vertical) 
f = Focal length of the lens 
D = Working distance 
 
For Technexion VCI-AR1335-CB camera: 
S = 4.8mm(W) * 3.6mm(H) 
f = 6.0mm 
D = 700mm (stakeholder requirement) 
 
By applying the formula above,

Appendix L: Understanding Object Detection [19] 
Object detection is the process of identifying and locating objects within an image. 
Unlike image classification, which only assigns a label to the entire image, object 
detection provides both the category of the object and its location. The output of an 
object detection model typically consists of bounding boxes around detected objects, 
along with a class label and a confidence score for each prediction. 
 
Figure L1. Depiction of Object Detection Output 
The model scans the image and predicts multiple candidate regions that may contain 
objects. For each region, it assigns a class label and estimates a bounding box that 
encloses the object. It then calculates a confidence score for each prediction. The final 
output is a set of bounding boxes, each with a predicted class and confidence, filtered 
using a threshold and techniques like Non-Maximum Suppression to eliminate 
duplicates.

Appendix M: Understanding Classification [20] 
Image classification is the process of assigning a single label or category to an entire 
image based on its content. A classification model analyzes the visual features within an 
image and determines the most probable class it belongs to from a predefined set of 
categories. Unlike object detection, classification does not provide information about the 
location of objects, only their presence. 
 
Figure M1. Image Classification Workflow 
 
The model processes the entire image and assigns a probability to each possible class. 
These probabilities indicate how likely the image belongs to each category. The class 
with the highest probability is selected as the final output. For example, if an image is 
evaluated as 80% likely to be a cat, 15% dog, and 5% rabbit, the model returns “cat” as 
the classification result.

Appendix N: Understanding Optical Character Recognition [21] 
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) is the process of detecting and extracting text 
from images or scanned documents. OCR systems analyze visual patterns to recognize 
characters, words, and lines of text, and convert them into machine-readable formats. 
Unlike classification or object detection, OCR focuses specifically on text data within 
visual content. 
 
Figure N1: OCR Applications 
 
OCR typically starts by detecting areas of the image that contain text. Once these text 
regions are identified, the image data is interpreted character by character or word by 
word. The model assigns a probability to each possible character at each position and 
selects the most likely sequence based on those probabilities. This output is then 
returned as plain text. Some OCR systems also use language models to correct spelling 
or improve accuracy based on context.

Appendix O: Understanding Transfer Learning [22] 
Transfer learning is a technique where a model pre-trained on a large, general dataset 
is adapted for a related but different task. Instead of training a model from scratch, 
transfer learning reuses the learned features from the pre-trained model, which helps 
reduce both training time and the amount of data needed. 
 
Figure O1: Transfer Learning Workflow 
Process of Transfer Learning 
1.​ Pre-training: The base model is trained on a large-scale dataset (e.g., ImageNet) 
to learn general features such as edges, textures, and shapes. 
2.​ Retraining the Classification Head: Since the pre-trained model’s original 
classification head is tailored to the source dataset classes, it must be replaced 
or retrained to predict the target task’s classes. This usually involves replacing 
the final layer(s) of the network with new layers appropriate for the target 
classification categories. 
3.​ Fine-tuning (Unfreezing Layers): Initially, most of the model’s layers are “frozen” 
(weights fixed) to retain the general features learned during pre-training. After 
training the new classification head, selective layers, often the later convolutional 
layers, are “unfrozen” to allow fine-tuning. This step adapts these layers to better 
capture domain-specific features in the target dataset, improving accuracy.

Appendix P: Understanding Early Stopping [23] 
Early stopping is a technique used during model training to prevent overfitting. It 
monitors the model’s performance on a validation set and stops training if the validation 
accuracy does not improve after a certain number of epochs, which is defined by the 
patience parameter.  
 
Figure P1: Early Stopping Graph 
 
Along with early stopping, best weight restoration can restore the model’s weights to 
those corresponding to the epoch with the best validation performance, rather than the 
weights from the final epoch. This ensures the model selected generalizes best to 
unseen data. 
 
Thus, using early stopping with patience and best weight restoration leads to more 
efficient training and better-performing models.
